Research & Learning Insights · Evidence Review

Virtual Reality for Second-Language Learning: A Systematic Review of the Evidence

What does immersive VR actually add to second-language learning—and when does the headset become little more than an expensive distraction? This review separates measurable language outcomes from presence, novelty and enjoyment.

62 scholarly sources synthesizedEvidence through August 2026Vocabulary · listening · speaking · writing · pragmatics · anxiety · retention

What does the evidence support?

Overall
Across meta-analyses, VR-assisted language learning shows a small-to-medium positive average effect compared with non-VR instruction, but effects vary substantially by task, comparison condition and implementation.124
Strongest case
Evidence is most coherent when VR supplies something conventional media cannot easily provide: situated vocabulary, interactive listening, scenario-based speaking, pragmatic rehearsal, spatial context and repeated low-stakes communicative practice.8171833
Not magic
Presence, immersion and enjoyment are not learning outcomes. VR can increase cognitive load, distract attention, create cybersickness or fail to outperform a well-designed non-VR alternative.535859
Best role
The most defensible use of VR is as a practice environment inside a broader language curriculum—not as a replacement for systematic instruction, retrieval, feedback, reading, writing, listening and human interaction.

01 · Executive summary

VR helps most when it changes the language task—not merely the display

The central question is not whether virtual reality is “immersive.” It is whether immersion changes what the learner can perceive, decide, say, hear, remember or rehearse.

Three quantitative syntheses now provide a reasonably stable high-level picture. Qiu et al. pooled 23 controlled EFL studies and reported a small positive effect (g = .445). Chen, Wang and Wang synthesized 21 studies with 1,144 participants and reported medium effects for both linguistic outcomes (g = .662) and affective outcomes (g = .570). A 2026 meta-analysis focused specifically on productive skills synthesized 21 studies with 2,503 learners and found a moderate effect (g = .538), but heterogeneity was high (I² ≈ 79.5%).124

That heterogeneity is not statistical clutter; it is the main lesson. “VR” includes everything from desktop virtual worlds and 360° scenes to six-degree-of-freedom head-mounted environments with interactive objects, avatars and speech agents. The same hardware can support a demanding communicative task or a passive tour. The same virtual scene can create useful contextual cues or overwhelm working memory.

Bottom line

VR is not a learning mechanism by itself. It is a delivery environment. Benefits arise when the environment creates useful contextual cues, requires language-relevant action, supports interaction, and connects those experiences to retrieval, feedback and later transfer.

02 · Definitions

“Virtual reality” is not one treatment

Language-learning papers frequently mix technologies that differ profoundly in sensory immersion and interactivity. That makes broad comparisons difficult. A head-mounted experience with tracked hands and spatial audio is not equivalent to a desktop 3D world, a 360° video, or a mobile panoramic scene. Recent reviews increasingly separate high-immersion HMD VR from lower-immersion virtual environments.815

01

Desktop / non-immersive virtual worlds

3D spaces viewed on a monitor, often with avatars and social interaction. These can still support situated tasks, collaboration and language exchange without strong bodily presence.

02

Spherical / 360° VR

Panoramic scenes viewed through a headset or mobile display. Useful for place-based context and perspective, but often less interactive than fully tracked VR.

03

High-immersion HMD VR

Head-tracked stereoscopic environments, often with controllers, hand tracking, spatial audio, avatars or AI agents. This is where claims about presence and embodiment are strongest—and where cognitive-load and cybersickness risks are greatest.

04

Social VR

Multi-user spaces in which language is used with other people or embodied avatars. The linguistic value comes primarily from interaction, turn-taking, pragmatics and social presence rather than visual fidelity alone.

03 · Review method

A structured evidence synthesis, with meta-analyses given the most weight

The review was designed to answer a decision question: what can a language educator reasonably expect from VR, and what still requires stronger evidence?

Searches targeted combinations of virtual reality, immersive VR, second language, foreign language, EFL, vocabulary, listening, speaking, writing, pragmatics, anxiety, retention, systematic review and meta-analysis. Priority was given to peer-reviewed meta-analyses, systematic reviews, randomized or controlled experiments and influential theory papers. The matrix below contains 62 works spanning reviews, experiments and mechanism studies.

This page is a structured research synthesis rather than a registered de novo PRISMA review. It does not claim exhaustive database coverage, duplicate screening or independent risk-of-bias adjudication. Instead, it triangulates the most relevant recent syntheses with representative primary studies and explicitly includes counterevidence.

Highest weightMeta-analyses, systematic reviews, randomized controlled trials.
Moderate weightControlled/quasi-experimental studies with objective language outcomes.
Contextual weightMixed-method, case, usability and classroom implementation studies.
Mechanism weightGeneral VR learning, memory, presence and cognitive-load studies used to explain—not prove—L2 effects.

04 · Overall effectiveness

The pooled effect is positive, but the field is too heterogeneous for “VR works” to be a sufficient conclusion

Qiu et al.’s EFL-specific meta-analysis reported g = .445, while Chen et al. reported g = .662 for linguistic outcomes and g = .570 for affective outcomes. Yu and Duan’s synthesis of 41 randomized controlled studies likewise found lower anxiety and higher motivation, performance, satisfaction and self-efficacy under VR-assisted conditions.123

More recent work narrows the claim. The 2026 productive-skills meta-analysis found g = .538, but effect sizes ranged from negative to very large and heterogeneity was substantial. Meanwhile, the most stringent K–12 review found only six eligible immersive-VR RCTs out of 1,054 initial records; vocabulary, listening and delayed retention looked promising, but all six trials had at least “some concerns” in risk-of-bias assessment.45

Interpretation rule

If a study compares VR with a dull worksheet, it may measure novelty + interaction + multimedia + task design + immersion all at once. Stronger studies hold content constant and vary only the feature of interest.

05 · Vocabulary & retention

Contextual vocabulary is one of VR’s most credible strengths

Vocabulary benefits are most plausible when the environment binds a word to an object, action, place, goal or dialogue—and later requires the learner to retrieve it.

Alfadil’s VR vocabulary game, Tai et al.’s adolescent Mondly study, Lai and Chen’s VR-versus-PC visual novel, Luan et al.’s purpose-built environment and Xie et al.’s input-processing intervention all report positive vocabulary outcomes under at least some measures.1618192122

The comparison with non-VR media matters. In Lai and Chen, both VR and PC groups learned vocabulary, while the VR advantage emerged on the delayed translation test. Tai et al. similarly found stronger learning and retention for interactive HMD VR than for watching a walkthrough video. These results support a narrower proposition: interactive spatial context may strengthen encoding and later retrieval, especially when it provides meaningful cues that a passive comparison lacks.1819

A key limitation is context dependence. Remembering a word inside the same virtual kitchen is not the same as producing it in a real kitchen, a conversation, a writing task or a test. Strong studies therefore need delayed tests and transfer tests outside VR.

06 · Listening & comprehension

VR can make listening consequential: learners hear language because they need it to act

Tai and Chen randomly assigned 72 seventh graders to an immersive Mondly condition or video-watching condition. The VR group showed stronger listening comprehension and retention. The authors linked the effect to authentic activity, prior-knowledge activation, inference, presence and reduced anxiety/cognitive overload.17

This is theoretically important because much classroom listening is detached from action. In real interaction, listeners use visual context, speaker cues, objects and goals to constrain interpretation. VR can recreate some of that multimodal ecology. But it can also add irrelevant visual information, locomotion demands and interface management that compete with speech processing.

01

Useful contextual constraint

Seeing a menu, map, person, room or object can narrow the set of plausible meanings and reduce ambiguity.

02

Attention to consequential input

If the learner must act on what was heard, listening becomes goal-directed rather than decorative.

03

Risk of split attention

Beautiful scenes, movement, menus and controls can compete with phonological decoding. A visually richer environment is not automatically a better listening environment.5859

07 · Speaking, pragmatics & anxiety

VR’s distinctive contribution may be rehearsal of social language under controlled pressure

For speaking, the strongest argument for VR is access: it can simulate interlocutors, audiences and situations that are difficult to stage repeatedly in a classroom.

Studies of oral communication, tourism English, pragmatic competence and public speaking show how virtual environments can make learners respond to socially meaningful cues rather than recite isolated sentences.31323334

Anxiety evidence is encouraging but nuanced. Kaplan-Rakowski and Gruber, Ding, and Park et al. report reductions or useful shifts in aspects of foreign-language speaking anxiety under high-immersion or repeated-exposure conditions. However, VR does not automatically feel safe: social presence and embodied audiences can also reproduce real anxiety, and individual differences remain substantial.353637

What VR can uniquely vary

Audience size, interlocutor behavior, background noise, social distance, time pressure, setting familiarity and task difficulty can be manipulated gradually. That creates a plausible graded rehearsal system for communicative confidence—provided learners receive language feedback, not merely exposure.

The 2026 productive-skills meta-analysis reports a moderate overall benefit for speaking/writing, but its heterogeneity means it would be an error to promise that a headset alone makes people fluent. Productive growth requires repeated output, feedback, lexical/grammatical support and opportunities to reuse improved forms.4

08 · Writing & collaboration

VR can supply experiences to write about—and shared problems to solve

Writing appears less intuitively “VR-native” than speaking, yet several studies show a coherent mechanism: immersive experiences generate content, perspective and situated knowledge that can later be organized into written language. Lan et al. used immersive Mandarin experiences to support writing; Khodabandeh found benefits for EFL paragraph writing; Guan et al. linked empathetic VR experience to writing performance; Mubarok et al. combined VR with collaborative argument mapping.30394041

This is a useful corrective to the idea that every learning action must occur inside the headset. The best VR lesson may end with the headset coming off. A learner can experience a scenario in VR, then complete retrieval, discussion, writing, reflection or assessment on a normal screen or on paper.

09 · Mechanisms

Presence is an affordance; learning still requires cognitive work

Makransky and Petersen’s Cognitive Affective Model of Immersive Learning (CAMIL) is useful because it separates technological affordances from learning processes. Immersion can increase presence and agency, which can influence motivation, embodiment and attention. But those states help only when they result in relevant cognitive processing.57

01

Situated cognition

Language is tied to places, objects, intentions and social roles. VR can recreate those contexts and therefore supply richer retrieval cues.

02

Embodied action

Reaching, pointing, navigating and manipulating can connect lexical meaning to action, but physical activity must remain relevant to the target language.

03

Social presence

Avatars and virtual characters can create turn-taking pressure, pragmatic cues and willingness-to-communicate opportunities.

04

Affect and agency

Choice, novelty and perceived safety can increase motivation or reduce anxiety. These are mediators, not substitutes for learning.

05

Contextual memory

Distinct virtual environments can become memory cues. This may improve retention, but transfer should be tested outside the original scene.6061

10 · Limits & counterevidence

More presence can also mean more distraction

General VR learning research gives a warning language educators should take seriously. Parong and Mayer found that immersive VR can produce strong presence and positive affect while also increasing extraneous cognitive load. Ahn et al. showed that spatial presence can have unintended consequences for learning when environmental immersion captures attention away from instructional content.5859

Language-specific counterevidence exists as well. Hung, Chen and Chien explicitly report that VR is not always a cure-all in EFL business speaking. Cowie and Alizadeh similarly conclude that language-learning acquisition evidence remains less definitive than promotional enthusiasm suggests, especially after accounting for cost, teacher training and cybersickness.5315

Recurring limitations across the evidence base

  • Small samples, short interventions and novelty effects.
  • Inconsistent definitions of VR and frequent mixing of desktop worlds, 360° video and HMD VR.
  • Weak active controls that do not match time-on-task, interactivity or multimedia exposure.
  • Heavy reliance on self-report motivation, presence and satisfaction.
  • Too few delayed retention and far-transfer measures.
  • Cybersickness, fatigue, headset fit, vision/comfort issues and classroom logistics.
  • Under-researched teacher role, facilitation burden and accessibility.
  • English-dominant evidence base with much less work in French and other target languages.7475556

11 · Design implications

A research-aligned VR language program should be built around tasks, not worlds

Eight defensible design rules

  1. Give every scene a language job. The learner should need to identify, understand, choose, ask, explain, respond or produce.
  2. Keep target language salient. Reduce decorative movement and irrelevant objects when they compete with listening, reading or lexical retrieval.
  3. Prefer short, repeatable scenarios. Multiple exposures are more defensible than a single spectacular session.8
  4. Connect VR to pre- and post-VR learning. Teach key language before immersion; retrieve, write, discuss and assess after it.
  5. Use interactivity purposefully. Clicking everything is not learning. Actions should expose meaning, trigger dialogue or require a decision.
  6. Build graded social pressure. For speaking, move from private rehearsal to virtual interlocutors, small audiences and more demanding scenarios.
  7. Measure delayed retention and transfer. Test whether learners can use the language later, without the headset and in new situations.
  8. Design for comfort and choice. Offer seated modes, short sessions, clear boundaries, hygiene procedures and alternatives for learners who cannot or do not wish to use HMDs.

13 · Conclusion

VR is most defensible as a simulator for language use

The evidence does not justify “VR makes language learning better” as a universal claim. It does justify a narrower and more useful one: well-designed virtual environments can improve some language outcomes by making vocabulary spatial and actionable, listening consequential, speaking repeatable, pragmatics contextual, and anxiety manageable. Average effects are positive, but highly variable. The educational value comes from what the learner must do with language inside the environment—and what they can still do after the headset comes off.
